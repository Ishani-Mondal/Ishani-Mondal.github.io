Teaching

I have taught across the full range of a computing curriculum — first-year programmers learning what an error message is for, and graduate researchers defending a design choice. What follows is how I think about the work, what I have taught, and what I would like to build.

Teaching Statement (PDF) CV

Coverage is not comprehension. A lecture, like a generated explanation, can name every correct topic in the correct order and transmit nothing. In our study of automatically generated research talks we found exactly this — models followed a paper's structure and never explained why the method worked. That is also the default failure of lecturing, and it is invisible from the front of the room, because the person explaining already holds the structure that students are still assembling.

How I teach

01 — Measurement

Instruments that measure understanding, not surface

My published work asks whether an evaluation detects comprehension or only fluency, and I hold assignments to the same bar. I use brief formative checks — a two-minute written response, an anonymous poll — so that every student commits to an answer before anyone speaks. Open questions to a room reliably surface the same three confident students and tell an instructor almost nothing.

I also treat rubric design as intellectual work rather than administration, a view I had to act on as Lead Teaching Assistant, where my grading reasoning needed to be explicit enough for several assistants to apply consistently.

02 — Audience

Audience is a variable, not a constant

My generation research rests on the premise that the same result requires a different telling for a reviewer, a student, and a clinician, and that what matters is expertise, goals, and cognitive load rather than demographic profile. A classroom is this problem in miniature, and pitching to the median serves nobody.

This shapes how I teach generative tools too. My own work found that experts adopt AI-extracted content substantially while remaining irreplaceable, and that language models are effective annotation assistants but poor independent annotators. Their failure mode is fluent, plausible wrongness — so I would rather grade a student's audit of a model's output than issue a prohibition.

03 — Access

Equity as a design constraint, not an afterthought

My research is largely about people that standard systems leave out: an annotation framework for multilingual HIV-positive youth in a peer support forum in Kenya, speech technology for Mundari, healthcare queries in Indic languages. The conviction underneath is that a system serving only the median user is unfinished, and a course is no different.

Students who arrive without the preparation their peers had are rarely less capable; they are learning the material and the discipline's tacit conventions at once, and only the first is on the syllabus. So I name the conventions explicitly — what counts as evidence, when a claim needs a citation rather than an experiment.

Courses I would develop

Evaluating generative systems. Students begin by breaking a published metric — constructing inputs on which it certifies something plainly wrong — then work through measurement design, annotation and agreement, human-grounded difficulty, and the failure modes of LLM-as-judge, before building and validating a measure of their own.

AI for scientific communication. A project course in which students take a real paper from a partner domain and produce a faithful explanation of it — a diagram, a deck, a narrated walkthrough — then design an evaluation and run it on readers from the intended audience. The deliverable is not the artifact but the evidence about whether it worked.
